# Plain Language Explainer - Privacy-Preserving Age Verification

This document explains why age verification does not need to require identity checks, ID uploads, or selfies.

It is written for non-technical readers, journalists, policymakers, and advocates.

---

## The issue

There is growing pressure to require age verification for:

- social media
- adult websites
- other online services "for the safety of children"

Protecting children online is a valid goal.

However, many proposed solutions push toward the worst possible implementation:

- uploading a driver's license or passport
- uploading a selfie
- sending highly sensitive identity data to private companies

This creates serious privacy and safety risks for everyone.

---

## The core idea

Age verification is not the same thing as identity verification.

To enforce an age rule, a website only needs to know:

- "Is this user over the required age?"

It does not need to know:

- who the user is
- their name
- their address
- their exact birthdate
- their ID number
- what they look like

---

## Why ID uploads are dangerous

If websites and verification vendors collect ID documents and selfies, the question is not:

- "Will this data leak?"

The question is:

- "When will it leak?"

Major technology companies with large security budgets are breached regularly.

If millions of people are forced to upload IDs for everyday internet use, the result is:

- the largest collection of identity documents ever assembled by private companies

This becomes a high-value target for criminals and extortion groups.

---

## What can go wrong if this data leaks

A leak of age verification records can enable:

- identity theft
- fraud and document forgery
- doxxing and stalking
- blackmail and extortion
- harassment
- forced outing of LGBTQ people
- permanent linkage between identity and browsing history

This risk is not hypothetical.

It is predictable.

---

## A better way exists

There are privacy-preserving ways to verify age that do not require identity disclosure to websites.

The key approach is:

- prove an attribute, not identity

Meaning:

- prove "over 18"
- without proving "who you are"

---

## What privacy-preserving age verification looks like

A safe system works like this:

1. A trusted issuer verifies the user once
   - Example issuers: government, bank, mobile carrier

2. The issuer provides a digital credential or token
   - The user stores it in a wallet (often on their phone)

3. When a website requests age verification, the user provides a proof
   - The proof only answers: yes/no (or age band)

4. The website receives no ID documents
   - No selfie
   - No birthdate
   - No name
   - No ID number

---

## Why this is better

A privacy-preserving system:

- prevents websites from collecting identity data
- prevents mass storage of IDs and selfies
- reduces breach impact
- reduces blackmail risk
- reduces cross-site tracking
- still supports child safety goals

---

## The hidden incentive problem

Many platforms and vendors prefer identity-based systems because identity data can be used for:

- tracking users across services
- advertising enrichment
- permanent bans and account linking
- moderation shortcuts
- creating centralized logs of online activity

This is why the worst implementation often becomes the default.

---

## The policy requirement that matters most

If age verification is required by law, the law must also require:

- minimum disclosure

Meaning:

- websites receive only a yes/no answer
- not identity data

If the law does not explicitly require this, the market will default to identity collection.

---

## The simplest test

If a website can say:

- "Upload your ID and a selfie to continue"

Then the policy has failed.

---

## Summary

Child safety and adult privacy are not in conflict.

Age verification can be implemented in a way that:

- protects children
- protects privacy
- avoids mass identity collection
- avoids creating surveillance infrastructure

The choice is not technical.

It is political and economic.

The safest option must be required, not merely allowed.
